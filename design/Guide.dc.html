Your skin barrier tends to be at its strongest — keep layers light.

Oestrogen peaks. In a small study, skin lost less water and held more around ovulation than later in the cycle. Whether oil rises now or before your period is unclear — studies disagree.

Home care
Simple, safe things to try at home in this phase.
Kaolin clay on an oily T-zone
Low risk, little evidence
Mix about 1 teaspoon of plain kaolin clay with water into a paste. Spread it thinly on your forehead, nose and chin, rinse after 5–10 minutes, then moisturise. Once or twice a week.
Clay soaks up surface oil.
It can dry skin — skip dry, broken or irritated areas, and patch test first.
Cooled green tea compress
Low risk, little evidence
Steep a plain green tea bag in just-boiled water for 3–5 minutes and chill it fully. Soak a clean cloth in it and hold it on oily or red areas for 5–10 minutes.
Green tea lotions reduced oil and spots in small trials. Brewed tea itself hasn’t been tested.
Stop if it stings.
Rinse after sweating
Low risk, little evidence
After exercise, gently cleanse or rinse your face with lukewarm water and pat it dry.
Dermatologists recommend cleansing after sweating as part of a gentle routine.
Pat dry — don’t rub.
Don’t try this at home
Popular online — and more likely to hurt your skin than help it.
Lemon or lime juice
Contains psoralens, which react with sunlight and can cause burn-like blisters and dark streaks.
Baking soda
It’s alkaline. Raising skin’s natural acidity disrupts barrier repair and helps bacteria grow.
Toothpaste on spots
Its abrasives, whitening agents and flavourings don’t fight acne and can irritate skin.
Undiluted essential oils, including tea tree
Neat oils can cause a lasting skin allergy — and old, oxidised oils are more likely to.
Neat apple cider vinegar
Its acetic acid has caused chemical burns, especially under a plaster.
Sugar or salt face scrubs
Scrubbing irritates skin and can make acne flare.
Hydrogen peroxide
It dries out and damages healing skin — dermatologists advise against it.
Garlic or cinnamon
Both have caused chemical burns on skin, and cinnamon also causes allergic rashes.
Heard online — not quite true
Common cycle-and-skin claims, checked against the research.
“Ovulation gives you a visible glow”
Facial redness rises slightly, but not enough for the eye to see.
“Everyone breaks out before their period”
Roughly half of women with acne do (44–65% across studies) — so many don’t.
“Switch products every phase”
No trials test phase-switching routines, and swapping acne treatments too often makes acne worse.
Worth knowing
Pregnant or trying to be? Avoid retinoids (retinol, retinal, adapalene, tretinoin, tazarotene) and hydroquinone — azelaic acid is generally considered a safe alternative. Breastfeeding? Check with a pharmacist before using a retinoid.
On the combined pill? It stops ovulation, so these phases may not match your skin.
See a pharmacist or GP if you notice
Acne with irregular periods, extra hair, thinning hair or weight gain — can point to PMOS (previously PCOS) · deep, painful lumps or early scarring · pharmacy treatment not helping after 6–8 weeks · three missed periods when you’re not pregnant · blistering or a spreading rash after a product.
How strong is the evidence?
Your cycle can nudge your skin, but no study yet shows that switching products by phase beats a steady, gentle routine. Treat these as timing tips, not rules.
