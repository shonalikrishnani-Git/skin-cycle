The full story

Skin Cycle started as a prototype built in Google AI Studio during an AI certificate, then was rebuilt from scratch in Claude Code as its own project. The direction changed more than once along the way — the honest version of that history is below, not a tidied-up one.

Skin first, not a period tracker

An early version tracked routine and mood alongside the cycle and read like a menstrual app with skincare bolted on. That was corrected: the cycle is context for the guidance, not the headline. Two ideas were also considered and rejected outright rather than built: "toxic ingredient" scores (toxicity depends on dose and route, which an ingredient list doesn't have) and scraped retail reviews (against most retailers' terms, and star ratings are the noise this app is meant to be an alternative to).

How the guidance was validated

Every sentence of health guidance was treated as wrong until a source someone actually opened — the AAD, NHS, HSE, DermNet, the FDA, or a published study — supported it. This happened in three rounds, and each one found real things to fix:

  • First pass. Cut honey (a randomised trial found it no better than soap for acne) and aloe vera (only two small studies, alongside other treatments, and it can cause rashes). Replaced ice on a deep spot with the AAD's current warm-compress advice. Downgraded oatmeal from "good" to "some evidence" (the studies were in eczema, not acne) and green tea to "little evidence" (lotions were tested; brewed tea wasn't). Removed an unsupported "drier and duller during your period" claim and moved "ovulation glow" to the myths list. Sources: 22 → 64.
  • Second pass found the first one had only checked the guidance file, so a wording slip in the cycle-phase code, a stray example in a text field, and the README itself had gone unchecked. Fixed, and the check was widened to cover everything on screen, not one file.
  • Third pass went wider still — every claim re-opened against its source a second time, plus a full repo grep for anything the earlier passes might have missed. It caught a source that was actually about a related but different compound (relabelled), a cellulitis warning that grouped a "call emergency services" sign in with a "see your GP" sign (split into the right tiers), and a breastfeeding note missing two ingredients that were already named elsewhere in the guidance (added, with two new sources). Sources: 64 → 66. This pass also added 243 automated tests that now check every remedy has a caution, every source is actually used, and this page's own numbers match the code — so the next change that quietly breaks one of those gets caught by a test, not by hoping someone checks by hand again.

What hasn't happened yet: review by an actual pharmacist or dermatologist. Every check above was done by AI validation agents opening real sources, not by a licensed professional — that's a real gap, not a formality, and it's why the app says plainly that it isn't medical advice.

Decisions worth knowing

  • Phases scale with your cycle — ovulation is counted back from the end of your cycle, so a 35-day cycle isn't treated like a 28-day one.
  • Routine steps, not a yes/no checkbox. Morning: cleanse, serum, moisturise, SPF. Evening: cleanse, treatment, moisturise — always in that order.
  • A day you never rated is never counted as "okay." The pattern feature stays quiet until there's enough real data to say something true.
  • Skin type actually changes the advice, not just a tip line underneath it — oily, dry, combination and sensitive skin see different tile wording, sourced from the AAD's own guidance on picking a moisturiser by skin type.
  • Accessible basics — a real page heading and landmark, no text under 12px, and two elements that measurably failed contrast now pass WCAG AA.

Built with

React 19, strict TypeScript, Vite, Tailwind 4. Everything stays in your browser's local storage — no account, no server, no API key, nothing sent anywhere.

Honest limits

  • One device, one browser. Clearing your site data erases everything.
  • "Skin Cycle" is a working title — other apps and skincare brands already use variations of the name, and "skin cycling" is an established trend of its own. A rename is still on the list.
  • General skincare information only — not medical advice, not a medical device, not contraception.
  • Phases are estimates from the dates you log, not measured.
  • The published research on skin changing across the cycle is genuinely thin. The app says so on screen rather than overstating what a handful of small studies can support.
  • New installs get eight weeks of clearly-labelled sample entries so the diary isn't empty on day one — removable in one tap.
